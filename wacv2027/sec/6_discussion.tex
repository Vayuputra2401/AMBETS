\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What alignment metrics can and cannot show.}
$\casfg$ and concept-discriminability measure correlation between an internal signal and annotated
concepts. A module can maximize both while contributing nothing to the prediction, and the
alignment loss trains precisely what these metrics score. Any claim that a concept layer makes a
model interpretable requires an intervention. We state this as the paper's transferable lesson
because it cost us: on the correlational evidence alone we had concluded the module worked, and
only the causal test reversed that reading.

\paragraph{Additive beats subtractive.}
Both failed repairs share a shape: they try to force a decoder that segments well without its
bottleneck to depend on it, by removing alternatives. The optimizer routed around both. Making the
routing a term of the output instead means causal influence is arithmetic, and no weight
configuration undoes it after the fact. We expect this to generalize to other concept-bottleneck
designs in dense prediction, though we have tested it only here.

\paragraph{Reported metrics that do not transfer.}
Three comparisons become invalid under Eq.~\ref{eq:direct} and we flag them rather than tabulate
them. Decoder Divergence, $1-\mathrm{Pearson}(\text{decoder},\text{routing})$, is circular: the
decoder output \emph{contains} the routing term by construction, so it measures how much the
refinement perturbs the routing, not independent agreement. Segmentation loss is not comparable
across additive and non-additive configurations, because the larger logit scale lowers
cross-entropy without improving the argmax. And V2's low off-target figure ($0.022$ vs.\ DIRECT's
$0.079$) reflects its near-zero effect rather than better precision---an inert module perturbs
nothing, including the things it should.

\paragraph{No controlled accuracy comparison.}
We did not train plain-backbone counterparts under our split, so we make \emph{no} claim that the
module is accuracy-neutral in absolute terms. The ${\approx}1$ Dice point in
Table~\ref{tab:repair} is a \emph{relative} cost measured against the same architecture with an
inert bottleneck, which is the quantity the trade-off argument needs, but it is not a statement
about the cost of adding the module to a plain network.

\paragraph{An unexplained interaction.}
Zeroing both CCR paths is \emph{less} damaging than zeroing the routing term alone (edema $0.801$
vs.\ $0.418$, Table~\ref{tab:necessity}). The decoder is trained to add to a routing base; stripping
the base while leaving the expert path appears to leave it miscalibrated, whereas zeroing both puts
it in a cleaner regime. We report this because it is real and we cannot yet explain it.

\paragraph{Resolution, granularity, and scope.}
The bottleneck is $16^3$, so the explanation is coarse and necrotic core---tens of tokens per
case---is the consistent weak spot across every metric. $K{=}4$ follows the BraTS protocol and
forces transition-zone tokens into one concept. We evaluate one dataset and one anatomy; the two
leaks are properties of the architecture family rather than of the data, but that expectation is
untested. Routing entropy is a per-voxel uncertainty signal but poorly calibrated (ECE $0.68$), and
plain segmentation confidence predicts model error better than any routing-derived signal we tried
($0.883$ vs.\ $0.786$), so we make no uncertainty claim.

\paragraph{What the explanation is, and is not.}
CCR explains at the level of clinical concepts---which expert processed each region, and with what
confidence---not the router's internal reasoning. Its advantage over a saliency map is that it is
concept-labeled, intrinsic, modular, and now demonstrably causal; it is not spatially sharper, and
the segmentation output itself remains a finer-grained concept map. What the routing adds is a
low-dimensional, inspectable, and \emph{manipulable} account of the concept decision that the
output does not provide about itself.

\paragraph{What a causal bottleneck offers a clinical reader.}
We are careful not to overstate this: we ran no reader study, and the claim that clinicians want
concept-level explanations is an assumption of this literature rather than a finding of ours. What
the result does change is the \emph{kind} of question the model can answer. A saliency map supports
only ``where did the model look''. A concept-aligned but inert bottleneck adds ``what did the model
call this region'', which the segmentation output already answers at higher resolution. A
\emph{causal} bottleneck additionally supports counterfactuals---``what would the prediction be if
this region were edema rather than necrosis''---answered by the model itself, in one forward pass,
rather than by a surrogate. Whether that capability is clinically useful is an open question we
think is now worth asking, because before this repair the counterfactual was unavailable in
principle: the model's answer did not depend on the concept assignment.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
All configurations differ from one another only in the switches of Sec.~\ref{sec:fixes}; the
backbone, losses, curriculum, optimizer, data split and seed are shared, so the comparisons in
Table~\ref{tab:protocol} isolate the architectural change. Every reported number is computed by the
released protocol scripts from committed per-case result files, and the intervention's identity
diagonal is recomputed on every run as a self-check---a non-zero diagonal invalidates that run's
matrix and is reported rather than silently absorbed. We release the module, the three switches,
and the three diagnostic tests, since the tests are the part we expect to outlive this particular
architecture.

\paragraph{Practical guidance.}
Three recommendations follow directly, for anyone placing a concept layer in a dense-prediction
network. \emph{First, run the intervention before reporting alignment.} It costs one forward pass
per concept pair per case---minutes on a single GPU---and it is the only test here that separated
a working design from a decorative one. \emph{Second, check where the bottleneck actually is.}
In a hierarchical encoder the deepest stage is not the one whose width matches the module; ours
sat at stage~2 of~5, with the two most semantic stages flowing around it, and no metric we
reported would have revealed that. \emph{Third, prefer additive to subtractive coupling.} Both
subtractive repairs we tried were absorbed by the optimizer, one of them while actively harming
expert diversity; the additive form cannot be absorbed because its influence is arithmetic. The
same three checks apply unchanged to any concept-bottleneck variant, and none of them requires
retraining.

\section{Conclusion}
We set out to make the routing decision the explanation, and found that the obvious implementation
produces a concept bottleneck that is beautifully aligned and causally inert: $\casfg$ up to
$0.96$, best-in-class concept discriminability, and a prediction that does not move when the
routing is overwritten. The cause is not degenerate experts but two architectural leaks that
alignment metrics cannot see. A linear probe on an unsupervised encoder reaches $0.81$, so the
discriminability advantage over saliency baselines was weaker evidence than it appeared. Making
the routing an additive term of the output raises causal control from $0.011$ to $0.636$ for
${\approx}1$ Dice point. Beyond this system the lesson is methodological: \textbf{a concept layer
is an explanation only if the model consults it, and demonstrating that requires intervening, not
correlating.}

The broader point is not that this particular module is now causal, but that we could not have
told the difference without asking. The configuration we would have published on the strength of
its alignment numbers is the same one whose routing the decoder ignores entirely, and no metric in
common use would have separated them. Intervention is cheap, it requires no retraining, and in our
case it was the difference between a claim and a correction.
